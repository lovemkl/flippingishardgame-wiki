The first half of Flipping is Hard is the game teaching you itself — five areas that install the mechanics, calibrate your falls and quietly prepare you for the harder upper mountain. This guide walks them as a sequence: what terrain logic each area runs on, where the difficulty spikes live, and which lines reward learning properly rather than surviving once. It is written to be read alongside the how-to-flip guide — the areas assume the mechanics, the mechanics need the terrain.

## The Opening Zone: Where Momentum Is Born

Every climber's first hours happen here, and the area earns its place by being generous. The terrain logic: wide, readable slopes with soft consequences — falls that teach momentum and landing without charging full price. The lines worth learning: the gentle climbing lines near the start, climbed three different ways on purpose (all-small flips, all-committed, deliberate mix) until momentum feels like a budget you manage rather than a force you experience. The difficulty spike to expect: the first genuinely narrow ledge, which is less a skill check than a fear check — the point where committed flips start feeling like the required vocabulary. Climbers who treat the opening zone as a tutorial to rush miss its real function: it is the only place in the game where falling is cheap, and cheap falling is the practice environment for the expensive kind.

## The Second Area: Landings Get Serious

The terrain shifts to more angular geometry — steeper inclines, more ledges, first real drops. The logic of the area is landing law: surfaces arrive at angles that no longer forgive the lazy arrival, and square landings stop being advice and become requirements. The lines worth learning: any route featuring repeated small ledges in sequence, because sequential landings are the skill this area exists to train — arriving square, spending momentum, immediately setting the next flip. The spike: the first slide-prone slope, where landing angle determines whether you grip or begin a long, educational descent. The habit to install here is the flip guide's stick-or-bail decision — the second area is where indecision starts costing real distance.

## The Third Area: The Recoveries Chapter

Falls become a theme. The third area's terrain logic introduces geometry designed to punish and reward recovery skill — drop-offs, awkward middle sections, and lines where some falling is simply part of the route. This is the area that separates climbers who practiced falling from climbers who only practiced climbing. The lines worth learning: the deliberate-practice drops — safe-ish falls taken on purpose, steering and landing, until falling feels handled. The spike: the first fall that genuinely threatens to undo an area's progress, which is the checkpoint-thinking page's introduction to real stakes. The encouraging truth about this zone: it is the steepest difficulty jump in the first half, and the areas after it climb more smoothly once recovery stops being scary.

## The Fourth Area: Pace and Rhythm

The terrain opens up — longer routes, more route choices, the first sense that the mountain offers options rather than a single line. The logic: rhythm. The fourth area rewards steady, repeatable flipping over heroic bursts — the climber who can do the same competent twenty flips in a row outclimbs the one who can do five great ones. The lines worth learning: the boring lines — the consistent, unglamorous routes that a tired climber can repeat. The spike: the pacing trap, a section that baits speed and punishes it; the lesson is the flip guide's momentum discipline wearing terrain clothes. By the top of the fourth area, most climbers own every mechanic the game teaches — which is exactly when the game is ready to start asking harder questions.

## The Fifth Area: The First Half's Summit

The fifth area is a graduation — terrain combining every logic so far (momentum sections, landing gauntlets, recovery drops, rhythm routes) into longer, more connected climbs. The lines worth learning: all of them, because variety is the test — the fifth area checks whether your skills transfer when the terrain stops announcing which skill it wants. The spike: length itself. This is where sessions get long and the mindset page's rules start earning their keep — the reset breath, the two-attempt rule, the end-on-success. Clearing the fifth area is the first half's real achievement: beyond it, the mountain's reputation begins to be personally verifiable, and the upper areas (mapped in the companion guide) are waiting with the falls that make the game's title feel earned rather than advertised.

## How to Use This Guide

Read the section for your current area before the session, not during — the middle of a fall is a bad classroom. Return to the how-to-flip drills whenever an area exposes a specific weakness: momentum problems mean slope drills, landing problems mean ledge drills, fall spirals mean the recovery practice and, more likely, the mindset rules. And log your own lines — the community's knowledge of these areas is compounding daily, and this wiki's maps update with it. The first half of the mountain is generous by design; climb it like the gift it is, and the second half will find you ready.
