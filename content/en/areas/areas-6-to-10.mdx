Past the fifth area, Flipping is Hard stops auditioning you and starts being itself. The upper half of the mountain is where the title's promise is kept in full: longer routes over harder geometry, falls that punish more of your progress, and terrain that assumes every mechanic from the first half is not just learned but owned. This guide maps areas six through ten in the same shape as the first-half guide — terrain logic, spikes, lines worth learning — with one extra ingredient the upper mountain demands: honesty about when to take breaks. Read it with the mindset page nearby; the upper climb is as much temperament as technique.

## Area Six: The Consolidation Test

The sixth area opens the upper mountain by verifying the lower one. Terrain logic: composite routes — momentum, landing and recovery demands arriving in quick succession, with less recovery ground between them. It plays like a final exam for the first half's skills, and climbers who cleared area five on momentum carry-over often meet their first real wall here. The lines worth learning: the conservative ones, deliberately — the upper mountain rewards routes you can repeat when tired, and area six is where you discover which of your lines survive fatigue. The spike: route length without checkpoints' mercy — the first routes long enough that the reset breath after falls stops being optional. Clearing six means your fundamentals hold under pressure; the mountain responds accordingly.

## Area Seven: The Technical Gauntlet

Where the game's precision vocabulary expands. Terrain logic: narrow features, unforgiving angles, geometry that asks for flips the first half never needed — tighter landings on smaller targets, momentum spent at exact moments, the committed flip becoming routine vocabulary rather than occasional tool. The lines worth learning: the ones you fall off — area seven's difficulty is the point, and the climber who treats its gauntlets as drills climbs eight faster than the one who treats them as obstacles. The spike: the first true bottleneck of the upper mountain, a section that will eat sessions and hand back skill. The mindset rules earn their keep here more than anywhere: two-attempt rule, downgrade move, and the reminder that the bottleneck is passable — someone worse than you has cleared it, which is the rage genre's most comforting sentence.

## Area Eight: The Long Fall Zone

The terrain logic of area eight is height itself: routes strung across serious drops, where falls that were setbacks lower on the mountain become tours of it. This is the area that made the recovery practice from area three either the best investment of your first half or the absence you now feel. The lines worth learning: the escape routes — deliberate fall lines from each section toward the flattest recoverable ground, scouted on purpose, because in the long-fall zone a steered fall to a known safe ledge is a technique, not a failure. The spike: psychological, not technical — the drops look worse than the climbing beneath them is, and the climber who trusts their recoveries climbs looser and better than the one defending against every fall. Area eight is where the game's emotional curriculum pays its dividend: comfort with falling, built deliberately, converts the scariest terrain in the game into merely the hardest.

## Area Nine: The Mountain's Answer

Penultimate areas exist to test everything at once, and nine does not disappoint. Terrain logic: all of it, interleaved — technical gauntlets into long-fall routes into rhythm climbs, at length, with the mountain's full vocabulary and none of its earlier mercy. The lines worth learning: your own, by now — area nine rewards the self-knowledge the previous eight areas built: which skills are your strengths, which lines suit your hands, when your sessions end. The spike: the summit glow effect — closeness degrades play, and the ninth area's exits visible above turn calm climbers into rushing ones. The countermeasure is the mindset page's oldest rule: climb the section in front of you. The summit does not exist yet; the flip you are mid-way through does.

## Area Ten: Back to Jeff

The final area is the game's victory lap and its last laugh simultaneously — hard enough to honor everything before it, ending at the goal the whole climb named: Jeff, waiting, at the top of the land of lost things, for a phone that flipped its way home. What to expect: the mountain's densest difficulty, the community's most-watched sections, and a summit whose emotional payoff is genuinely proportionate to the falls it cost. What to bring: every habit this wiki teaches, warmed up — and, if you can arrange it, witnesses. The co-op guide's finest hour is the summit hour: thirty climbers watching the last ledge is the genre's best scene, and clearing it alone with no one watching is its most private. Both are real; most climbers choose company. Clear it, watch the land of lost things from the top, and understand why a game this hard about a phone this small made people this happy: because the difficulty was never the point. The climb back was.
