Rotation is the only verb Flipping is Hard gives you, which makes this page the most important one on the wiki. Everything else — the areas, the co-op, the comparisons — assumes you can flip, and flipping well is a learnable skill with three components: momentum, landings and recoveries. This guide covers each properly, with the drills that install them, the habits that stop small falls from becoming long ones, and the mental model that turns a phone-shaped hero into a precision instrument. Read it once before your first session and once after your first hundred falls.

## Momentum: How Flips Become Distance

Flippy is a physics object before anything else, and momentum is the currency the whole game runs on. A flip does two things at once: it rotates you, and it translates you — and the art of flipping is making the translation serve the climb. Small, quick flips keep momentum controllable; long, committed flips buy real distance at the price of real consequences. The beginner's default should be small: a series of controlled nudges that accumulate height without ever committing Flippy to a fall he cannot arrest. The expert's game is knowing when the situation has earned the committed flip — the gap that only momentum crosses, the slope that only speed climbs — and executing it without hedging.

The drill that installs this: pick a gentle slope in the opening area and climb it three ways — all tiny flips, all committed flips, and a deliberate mix. Feel how the tiny-only version stalls, how the committed-only version overshoots and slides, and how the mix carries. That feeling — when to spend momentum and when to bank it — is the entire skill's core, and ten minutes on a safe slope teaches it better than an hour of falling off harder terrain. Flipping is Hard rewards this practice generously: the physics are consistent, the causes readable, and every momentum mistake you make in practice is one you stop paying for on the mountain. And the reward compounds — the flip sense you build on the opening slopes is the same sense the tenth area will demand at full price, which makes these early drills the cheapest tuition the game will ever offer you.

## Landings: The Angles That Stick

If momentum is the currency, landing angle is the exchange rate. Flippy sticks surfaces at some angles and slides off them at others, and the difference between a good climb and a long fall is usually one thing: whether you met the ground flat or met it tilted. The habit to build is finishing every flip early enough to square up — rotating through the flip and arriving at the surface with Flippy's face parallel to it, momentum spent, ready to start the next. The classic Flipping is Hard beginner error is flipping late and landing mid-rotation: a phone that arrives tumbling cannot grip anything, and tumbling phones fall exactly as far as physics says they should.

Two landing rules cover most terrain in Flipping is Hard. On flat and gentle surfaces, arrive square and slow — banked momentum you cannot spend is just fall insurance you are carrying. On slopes and awkward geometry, aim to land slightly into the incline — the angle that lets the first contact arrest your slide before it starts. And on every landing, immediately decide: stick or bail. The recoverable landing that becomes an unrecoverable fall is almost always a stuck moment of indecision — the player who neither commits to the grip nor bails to a safer line watches the physics decide for them, and the physics has no favorites. Decisiveness is a landing skill as much as angle is.

## Recoveries: Falling Without Losing

The separation between good climbers and frustrated ones is not fall prevention — everyone falls, forever, at every skill level — but fall response. The recovery discipline has three parts. **Read the fall early**: the moment a landing fails, you usually have a beat to choose between arresting the slide (quick counter-flips against the direction of travel) and steering it (converting the fall into controlled travel toward the flattest available ground). The worst option is the default one — doing nothing while momentum compounds. **Steer while falling**: a tumbling Flippy still responds, and the difference between a two-meter drop and a twenty-meter drop is often two mid-air corrections. **Land ready to move**: arrives square, however briefly, because landings during recovery obey the same angle laws as landings during climbing.

The drill: deliberately fall. Find a mid-height drop in an early area and throw Flippy off it repeatedly, practicing the steer-and-land response until falling feels handled rather than catastrophic. This sounds defeatist; it is the opposite. Climbers who have practiced falling stop fearing it, and climbers who stop fearing it stop rushing — and rushing, in Flipping is Hard, is the cause hiding behind nearly every long fall. The calm climber who accepts small drops reads the mountain; the panicked climber avoiding all drops makes the big one inevitable.

## The Habits That Stop Fall Spirals

Fall spirals — one fall leading to a worse attempt leading to a worse fall — are the game's real final boss, and they die to habits rather than skill. **The reset breath**: after any fall past a checkpoint, one breath before the next input, no exceptions — it costs a second and saves minutes. **The same-line rule**: after a fall, take the same successful route back to the failure point rather than improvising; improvisation while annoyed is how small falls become tours of the map's lower third. **The two-attempt rule**: two failed attempts at the same obstacle earns either a different line or a break — the obstacle will still be climbable in ten minutes, and you will be better at it. And **the session-end checkpoint**: quit on a success, any success, because the memory your next session starts from should be the one where the flip worked.

## Flip, Fall, Climb

That is the entire control scheme and the entire skill ceiling: momentum you learn to spend, landings you learn to square, recoveries you learn to steer, and habits that keep the falls from writing the session's story. Flipping is Hard gives you one verb and then asks for a mountain — which sounds cruel until you feel the verb become yours, somewhere in the second or third session, when a flip you didn't think about lands exactly right and the climb opens up. Every guide on this wiki assumes that moment is coming for you; the areas guides will tell you where to spend it, the co-op guide will tell you who to share it with, and the mountain will be exactly as hard as the title says — and exactly as climbable.

## The Learning Arc, Timed

Because new climbers reasonably want to know how long the verb takes to become theirs, the launch-week arc looked like this: the first session is mostly falls — expect double-digit tumbles in the opening area alone, and treat every one as tuition rather than failure. The second session is usually where momentum clicks — the budget metaphor stops being a metaphor and starts being a felt sense, and the small-flip vocabulary stops requiring attention. The third and fourth sessions belong to landings — square arrivals become the default rather than the exception — and somewhere in the fifth or sixth, most climbers notice the strangest thing: they are reading the terrain ahead of time, pre-choosing angles before the flips start. That is mastery arriving, and Flipping is Hard has been quietly pacing its first areas to deliver exactly this arc to everyone who puts in the sessions.

The arc's encouragement for the frustrated middle: the wall almost every climber hits around landings is real, temporary and universal — the community's shared experience is that it breaks not with practice volume but with practice quality, which is why the drills above emphasize deliberate variation (three ways up one slope) rather than repetition volume. Ten focused minutes beats an hour of autopilot, and the climber who respects that difference reaches the upper mountain weeks earlier than the one who grinds. The verb becomes yours at the speed of your attention, not your hours — one of the quieter kindnesses in the game's design, and one worth knowing before the grind instinct takes over.
