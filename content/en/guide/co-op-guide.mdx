The headline feature nobody expects from a rage game: Flipping is Hard supports up to 32 players in online co-op, which means thirty-two phones tumbling up the same mountain at the same time — witnessing each other's falls, blocking each other's lines, and producing a comedy that solo climbing cannot match. This guide covers the co-op experience honestly: what scale actually changes about the game, how to climb together without ruining each other's runs, what co-op is genuinely for, and which mode to learn in. It assumes you have read how to flip; the mechanics are the same, the mountain is not — and neither is the mood, which is the entire point of pressing join.

## What 32 Players Actually Changes

Mechanically, the climb remains yours: your Flippy, your flips, your falls. Co-op does not attach ropes between players or share physics — it populates the mountain. What that population changes is everything around the climbing in Flipping is Hard. **Falls become shared**: the ledge that eats you has eaten someone visible nearby, is currently eating someone else, and will eat a third while you watch — and somehow this is medicine, because rage games are hardest to endure alone. **Lines become visible**: watching another player's route up a hard section is real-time route advice, the community's collective knowledge performing itself live. **The pace becomes social**: a solo Flipping is Hard session is a meditation; a co-op session is a party on a cliff, with all the starting-and-stopping, waiting-and-racing that implies.

The honest caveat: co-op can make individual sections harder. Thirty-two phones occupy space, and on narrow lines, other climbers are terrain — sometimes helpful terrain to bounce ideas (and occasionally Flippy) off, sometimes the reason your perfect landing landed on someone's edge. The game's chaos is largely benign, but climbers chasing personal-best runs will eventually want the mountain to themselves, which is why the solo-versus-co-op question below has a real answer rather than a shrug. The other honest note from launch week: Flipping is Hard's servers handle the crowd well — the physics stay consistent at full population, and the comedy scales with the headcount, which is not something the genre has been able to say before this game tried it.

## Climbing Together Without Ruining Each Other

The etiquette that keeps 32-player sessions fun is short and learnable. **Spread on narrow lines**: when the route pinches, take turns or take different lines rather than stacking — a queue at a hard ledge is comedy, a pileup is just a pileup. **Don't hover on someone's landing zone**: the climber above you cannot see below themselves; give landings room and expect the same. **Share lines by example, not lecture**: in text-and-emote communication, showing a route speaks louder than explaining it — take the line, make it look possible, and let the mountain's population learn by watching, which is the co-op mode's entire educational superpower. **And celebrate the falls**: the genre's culture at its best is that a spectacular tumble is content, and the group that laughs at falls together climbs longer than the group that rages alone.

For groups of friends specifically: the sweet spot for a planned Flipping is Hard session is small-to-medium — three to eight players is enough crowd for the comedy and little enough traffic for the climbing. Full 32-player servers are their own event — closer to a festival on a mountain than a climbing session — and best entered with summit ambitions switched off and spectacle expectations switched on. The in-between sizes have their own character too: a dozen climbers is roughly the scale at which the mountain starts feeling inhabited — routes dotted with phones above and below you, someone always mid-fall somewhere in view — while remaining small enough that the crowd learns each other's habits. Whichever size your group lands on, the session you get is the one you calibrate for: enter Flipping is Hard co-op with climbing goals and you will leave with comedy; enter with comedy goals and the climbing will surprise you by arriving anyway.

## Solo Versus Co-op: Which to Learn In

The recommendation, calibrated by what each mode teaches. **Learn the flip in solo**: the opening hours need focus, repetition and zero audience — momentum and landing habits install fastest when every fall is information instead of performance. The how-to-flip drills are solo drills by design. **Learn the mountain in co-op**: once the basics hold, the fastest route-knowledge in the game is following better climbers up real terrain — their lines, their pacing, their recoveries, all visible, all learnable. **Test yourself in solo**: the personal-best run, the no-checkpoint-fall attempt, the clean climb — these are solo projects, and the mountain's honest measurement happens without witnesses. **Celebrate in co-op**: the summit with thirty witnesses, or the shared agony of a shared ledge — this is what the mode is for.

The general rhythm that works in Flipping is Hard: solo early, co-op in the middle, solo for the record, co-op forever after. The mountain does not change between modes, but the climber does, and matching mode to mood is half of enjoying a rage game for the long haul. One practical note for planners: the modes reward different session lengths too — solo sessions are short-and-focused by nature (the attention a rage game demands is expensive), while co-op sessions stretch comfortably into evenings because the crowd carries the energy between your attempts. Schedule accordingly, and let the two halves of the game — the meditation and the party — take the slots in your week that fit each best.

## Why Co-op Belongs in a Rage Game

It is worth ending on why this feature is more than a checkbox. The Foddian lineage has always been about the psychology of difficulty — Getting Over It's narrator said it outright: the game is about the feeling of loss and recovery. Flipping is Hard's co-op takes that psychology social: recovery is faster with witnesses, loss is funnier with company, and the summit — any summit, any ledge, any single hard-won meter — lands differently when it lands in front of people watching you earn it. Thirty-two players on a mountain does not dilute the rage; it dilutes the loneliness, which was always the part that actually hurt. Bring friends for the falls. The climbing you can do alone; the laughing is better shared.

## The Sessions That Work

For groups planning their first co-op evenings, the session shapes that launch week validated. **The tutorial party**: everyone new, first area, no summit ambitions — the goal is shared falls and shared laughter, and this session teaches more mechanics per hour than any solo equivalent because thirty demonstration runs are happening simultaneously around you. **The mixed-ability climb**: one or two experienced climbers leading a group of newcomers up through the early areas — the guide-climber shows lines by taking them, the group learns by watching, and the mountain becomes a classroom with a soundtrack of tumbling phones. **The summit attempt**: an experienced group, full-serious, sections divided and paced together — the rarest and sweetest of the co-op shapes, worth saving for when the group's hands are ready. And the unofficial fourth shape, which the community invented on day one: **the summit watch** — one climber attempts the final area while everyone else spectates, cheers and memorializes the falls. That last one has no mechanical justification at all, and it is somehow the mode's finest hour; a game about a lost phone found its way to the genre's warmest scene, and the people who were there will tell you about it.
